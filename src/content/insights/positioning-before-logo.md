A logo is a compression format. It takes a set of decisions the business has already made and reduces them to a mark people can recognise in a quarter of a second. Compression is lossless only if there was something there to compress.

When a logo comes first, there is nothing to compress. So the designer substitutes taste, the client reviews on taste, and both parties end up arguing about whether the blue is right. Neither has a way to settle it, because there is no agreed standard the mark is being measured against.

## What positioning actually is

It is not a tagline. It is four decisions:

1. **Who this is for.** Specifically, and at the cost of excluding people.
2. **What it replaces.** Every purchase displaces something, even if that something is doing nothing.
3. **Why it is credibly better at that.** Credibly is the operative word. Something you can evidence.
4. **What you are willing to be worse at.** Positioning without a trade-off is a wish list.

Four answers. They fit on an index card. They take about a week of real work to produce, and most of that week is spent on the fourth one.

## Why the order matters

Once those four answers exist, a surprising number of design arguments resolve themselves.

Should the mark be playful or serious. That is no longer a matter of preference. It depends on whether the audience is buying reassurance or buying energy.

Should the palette be restrained or loud. Depends what you are displacing. If you are replacing an incumbent that people find cold, warmth is strategy. If you are replacing one people find unserious, restraint is.

Should the logo have a symbol or be a wordmark. Depends how the brand will be encountered. A business discovered through search and read on a website has different requirements to one glimpsed on a vehicle at forty kilometres an hour.

None of these are design questions. They become design questions only after they have been answered as business questions, and that is the whole argument for doing positioning first.

## How to tell it was skipped

You can usually spot it from outside.

- **The brand sounds like its competitors.** Not similar. Interchangeable. Swap the logos on the three sites and nobody notices.
- **Revisions have no end condition.** Round four of a logo, each version defensible, no way to choose. That is not indecision. It is the absence of a standard.
- **The tagline describes the category, not the business.** "Quality you can trust." "Your partner in growth." These appear when there is nothing specific to say.
- **The brand guidelines are entirely visual.** Colours, fonts, clear space, no voice, no audience, no positioning. A document that tells you how to apply a decision nobody wrote down.

## The objection, and the honest answer

Clients push back on this for a reasonable reason. They came with a concrete need, a logo, and are being offered an abstract one, a week of talking. It feels like the agency manufacturing scope.

Sometimes it is. Here is the test: positioning work should produce decisions, not documents. If you finish the week with a forty slide deck and no clearer idea of who you are selling to, you were sold a process. If you finish with four sentences you can repeat from memory, and at least one of them makes you slightly uncomfortable because of what it rules out, it worked.

The discomfort is diagnostic. Positioning that excludes nobody has decided nothing.

## What it looks like in practice

On the Floks Speaks project, the positioning question was not what a spoken word artist's site should look like. It was what the site is for. Is it a portfolio for bookers, a release channel for listeners, or a home for an audience that already follows the work.

Those produce three different websites. Answering it first meant the hero could put a featured track above everything else, with its title and runtime visible before any navigation. That decision is not a design preference. It falls directly out of the answer, and it is why the page holds together.

## The practical version

If you are briefing an identity project, bring the problem rather than the deliverable. Instead of "we need a logo and a website", try:

> We are losing enquiries to a competitor who is more expensive than us, and we think it is because we look newer than we are.

That is a brief. It names the audience, the displacement, the suspected cause and the standard for success. A designer can work with it. "We need a logo" gives them nothing but taste, and taste is not a shared language.

The logo still gets made. It just arrives last, and when it does there is a reason it looks the way it does.
